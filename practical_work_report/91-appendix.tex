\chapter{Documented Deviations from the Paper}
\label{app:an-appendix}

The paper's description of both experiments leaves a number of
implementation details either unstated or ambiguous, and this project
predates its exact scope with one deliberate substitution of its own. This
appendix collects every point at which the reproduction's implementation
necessarily deviates from a literal reading of the paper, together with
the reasoning behind the specific choice made and, where applicable, how
the choice was calibrated against numbers or figures the paper does
report. Cross-references point back to the chapter that introduces the
relevant context.

\begin{description}
  \item[Deep learning framework (Section~\ref{sec:problemStatement}).]
  The paper's reference implementation uses TensorFlow/Keras. This project
  substitutes PyTorch and JAX, run side by side, as a deliberate project
  constraint rather than an oversight; the PyTorch/JAX framework
  comparison in Section~\ref{sec:jaximplementation} is itself part of the
  intended deliverable. Because Keras' \texttt{HeUniform} initializer is
  activation-agnostic, both reimplementations had to be adjusted so that
  their own (activation-aware) default Kaiming/Xavier conventions matched
  Keras' behavior instead (Section~\ref{sec:pytorchreimplementation}).

  \item[Diffusion mesh generator (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).]
  The paper's Fig.~4 shows a visibly non-uniform, apparently unstructured
  mesh. The natural way to reproduce it, FEniCS's \texttt{mshr} mesh
  generator, is not usable in the installed legacy-FEniCS environment due
  to a \texttt{pybind11} ABI incompatibility between \texttt{mshr} and
  \texttt{dolfin} that could not be resolved by reinstalling through
  either \texttt{conda} or \texttt{mamba}. A structured
  \texttt{UnitSquareMesh} is used instead, with its resolution calibrated
  so that the resulting degree-of-freedom count and mesh spacing are as
  close as achievable to the paper's reported values.

  \item[NSB finite element family (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).]
  The paper's own appendix does not spell out the exact finite element
  family used for the four-field Navier--Stokes--Brinkman mixed
  formulation. The element family used here ($BDM_1$ pseudostress, $DG_0$
  velocity and skew vorticity, $DG_1$ trace-free strain) is taken from
  Gatica, N\'{u}\~{n}ez and Ruiz-Baier \cite{gatica2022nsb}, the paper's
  own cited source for this formulation, and calibrated against the
  degree-of-freedom counts the paper reports for this experiment.

  \item[NSB inlet velocity profile (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).]
  The inlet velocity formula as printed in the paper is degenerate at one
  corner of the inlet edge. An initial literal substitution produced a
  severe, unphysical pressure spike at that corner, visible as a sharp
  artifact inconsistent with the paper's own Fig.~6. Reflecting the
  formula so that it vanishes at the wall corner instead of the outlet
  corner removes the artifact and produces a clean, circulating flow
  pattern that visually matches Fig.~6; this reflected form is used
  throughout.

  \item[NSB pressure gauge (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).]
  The paper's formulation fixes the pressure field's additive constant
  through a Lagrange-multiplier constraint on the trace of the pseudostress
  $\sigma$, which was not implemented. Instead, the pressure field
  computed from $\sigma$ and $u$ is shifted by its own mean after solving,
  enforcing $\int_\Omega p = 0$ exactly by construction rather than through
  the constrained variational formulation itself.

  \item[Sparse-grid level (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).]
  The paper does not state a numerical sparse-grid level for its
  deterministic Clenshaw--Curtis test protocol. The level used for each
  case was chosen empirically to balance test-set resolution against the
  combinatorial growth of sparse-grid points at $d=8$, and is recorded in
  that case's generated dataset metadata.

  \item[NSB training epoch budget (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).]
  The paper's stated training protocol -- up to 60{,}000 epochs, stopping
  once the training loss falls below an absolute tolerance of
  $5\times10^{-7}$ -- is used unchanged for the diffusion experiment, where
  most runs converge well before the epoch cap. For NSB it is not: direct
  measurement (one task's loss trace showed $6.5\times10^{-6}$, still
  decreasing but far from the tolerance, after 11{,}000 of 60{,}000 epochs)
  showed that a large fraction of NSB runs -- particularly the larger
  architecture and larger training-set sizes -- need tens of thousands of
  epochs under the exponentially-decaying learning rate to approach
  $5\times10^{-7}$, and many effectively consume the full 60{,}000-epoch
  budget. Timed directly on this machine, the full NSB paper matrix
  (16{,}128 training runs) at that budget projected to roughly nine days of
  wall-clock time even with 16-way process parallelism, which is not
  tractable within the scope of this practical work. For the NSB experiment
  only, the epoch cap is reduced to 20{,}000 and the loss tolerance loosened
  to $5\times10^{-6}$ (configs/train/pytorch\_nsb.yaml,
  configs/train/jax\_nsb.yaml); every other element of the training
  protocol (optimizer, learning-rate schedule, checkpoint/restore rule,
  batch size, per-trial seeding) is unchanged. This is applied uniformly
  across all twelve trials of every NSB configuration, so that the
  geometric-mean/std statistics reported in
  Chapter~\ref{sec:experimentalresults} are not a mix of two different
  training budgets within the same cell.
\end{description}
